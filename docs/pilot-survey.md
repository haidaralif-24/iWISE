# Pilot Survey (target: 10-20 classmates)

Twenty minutes, ten people, in a room with a projector. The point is to get **real numbers** —
even small ones — so the pitch can say what actually happened rather than what should happen.

## How to run it

1. Pick one document everyone has: a worksheet, a set of notes, a report. The same one for
   everybody, or the results are not comparable.
2. **First**, time each person doing the task the old way: open the file, select the text, copy
   it, paste it into the chat, wait for the answer. Write down the minutes.
3. **Then**, show Pip once — thirty seconds, no explanation of the internals — and time the same
   task again.
4. Fill in the sheet below while they are still in front of you. Ask them to say the answers
   out loud; typing is where the honest answers go.
5. Ask the last question last. It is the one that matters.

Run the same task twice per person, not once. The second time is usually much faster, and that
difference is learning, not Pip.

## What to do with the numbers

- **Show them even if they are unflattering.** A pilot that shows a 20% saving and one
  participant who hated it is more credible than one that shows nothing, and the judges will ask
  whether there was a pilot.
- **Do not turn the token figure into "it saves tokens".** The app reports three numbers, and the
  honest statement is the one in the abstract: the conversion does not make the text smaller; page
  selection and editing do. Report the tokens before and after *the whole task*, including the
  trimming.
- **A person who says "I would not use it" is worth more than a person who says "it is great".**
  Write the criticism down verbatim. It is the most useful line in the whole exercise.
